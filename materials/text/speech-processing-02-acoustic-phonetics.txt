Sound waves

Sound waves in air
Air particles bounce back and forth at different frequencies: we observe a ‘wave’ of compression (and rarefaction) travelling through the air

https://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/Demos/waves/wavemotion.html

## Slide 40

Sound source: voiced, vowel
If we look at vowels instead, we can see periodic structure more clearly. This comes from the periodic opening and closing of the vocal folds
  
In the pink window we see 5 cycles of the repeating pattern. 

The length of each cycle is the period (t) of the wave and (for sound waves) is measured in seconds. 
The number of cycles completed each second corresponds to the frequency (f) and is measured in Hertz (Hz)
You can calculate the frequency from the period and vice versa: 
 f = 1/t

## Slide 50

Vowel Quality: Formants
We can distinguish vowels by their resonances → formants (aka “overtones”)
The 1st formant (F1) aligns with tongue height: high vowels have lower F1, low vowels have higher F1 (inverse relationship)
The 2nd formant (F2) aligns with frontness: front vowels have higher F2, back vowels have lower F2

## Slide 62

Take-aways
We can find acoustic cues to speech articulations by looking at the frequency spectrum of speech across time, i.e. spectrograms
We saw distinct cues for different manner and place of articulation in the spectrogram
We can attribute these to differences in source (e.g. vocal fold vibration, turbulent airflow) and filter (vocal tract shape)
Vocal tract resonances (i.e., formants) tell us about the shape of the filter
Though we can see systemic differences in speech sounds, actual acoustic realisations are highly variable -> we need statistical methods to model this variability
